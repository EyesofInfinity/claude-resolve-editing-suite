# Picture: shots, framing, camera and light

## Reading the footage first

Before planning, make a contact sheet of every clip (see [review.md](review.md)) and write one line on each: size, subject, light,
movement, quality. Reason over those lines when choosing shots. Describing clips in text first is the method that works best for AI
editors. [practice]

Pick takes in this order: performance and meaning, sound, focus, framing, exposure. A slightly soft shot of the true moment beats a
sharp shot of nothing.

## Composition

- Give the frame one subject. If you cannot name it, the viewer will not find it.
- Place subjects off centre on a third when the shot has room. Centre them on purpose, for symmetry, direct address or power.
- Keep eyes in the upper third, with a small gap above the head. A lot of air above the head makes a person look small.
- Leave room in the direction a person looks or moves.
- Keep the horizon level unless the tilt means something.
- Use depth: something near, the subject, something far. A foreground shape gives a flat shot space.
- Lines, doorways and light can lead the eye to the subject.
- Plain or darker backgrounds separate the subject. Clear anything growing out of a head.
- Plan empty space where text or a graphic will go.
- A portrait crop that works for a phone is a new composition. Reframe each shot for 9:16. Resolve's Smart Reframe follows the subject: check its result on the frames. Centre-cropping a wide film loses the subject.

## Shot sizes

- Wide shows where we are. Medium shows what is happening. Close shows what it means. Extreme close shows what matters most.
- Change size or angle meaningfully between two shots of the same subject: at least about 30 degrees of angle or a clear change of size.
  A small change reads as a mistake. [practice]
- Sequences often go wide to close as we learn more. Going from close to wide makes a reveal.
- Keep people on the same side of the frame across a conversation. Stay on one side of the line between them, the 180 degree rule. Eyelines
  should meet across a cut. [practice]
- Close-ups read on a phone and wide shots lose detail there. Use more close shots on short form.

## Moving the camera

- A move needs a reason: reveal, follow, or bring us closer.
- Most moves should barely be noticed. Ease in and out. Slower for big subjects and wide shots.
- A push toward a subject raises attention. A pull away ends a moment or reveals context.
- Vary moves across a film. The same slow zoom on every shot is the most common sign of an automated edit.
- A still frame can be the strongest choice, especially after a run of movement.
- Stabilise only what needs it, with Resolve's Stabilizer. Heavy stabilisation warps faces and crops the frame. A little human handheld feels true in a vlog or documentary.

## Moving a still photograph

- Slow drift, a few percent across several seconds. Under about 5 percent over 8 seconds reads as calm. [default]
- Move toward what matters in the picture. End on a good composition.
- For depth, separate foreground and background and move them at different speeds. Keep the shift small so the edges stay clean.

## Scale and punch-ins

Footage shot in 4K can scale to 200 percent on a 1080p timeline with no loss. 1080p footage softens past about 115 to 120 percent. [default] Sharpening
cannot bring that detail back, so choose the wider shot and keep the moment.

## Frame rate and shutter

- Pick the timeline rate before the edit and keep it. 24 reads as film, 30 as video and web, 60 as sport, screens and smooth motion graphics. Animated
  motion graphics in this suite render at 60 with motion blur.
- A shutter at about twice the frame rate (1/50 at 24 or 25, 1/60 at 30) gives natural motion blur. [practice]
- High-rate footage on a lower timeline gives real slow motion. Conform it. Retimed footage needs optical flow, which can smear water, grass and leaves.
- Motion graphics effects that count time may need their speed lowered on faster timelines: see `EFFECTS.md`.

## Light

This is the part of a shot that cannot be fixed later. Read it on every clip.

- Light from the side or at 45 degrees shapes a face. Flat front light looks like a phone flash. Light from directly overhead puts shadows in the eyes.
- A large soft source gives flattering skin, and a window is the best free one. Hard small sources give drama and texture.
- A background a little darker than the face makes the person stand out. A separate rim or a lit background adds depth.
- Mixed colours of light on one face (window and warm bulb) are hard to grade. Choose one, or use the mix on purpose.
- A shot with a blown sky or a clipped face has lost that detail. Choose another take, or crop, or let the sky go and protect the face.
- Mood is mostly contrast. A low-key frame with deep shadow feels serious or private. A high-key frame with few shadows feels open and light.

## When the user has not filmed yet

Good advice now saves every later stage. Give a short plan and a shot list.

- **Voice first.** A cheap microphone close to the mouth, 15 to 30 cm, beats an expensive one across the room. A quiet soft room matters more
  than the microphone. Record 30 seconds of room tone. [practice]
- **Window light.** Face a window, or stand with it at 45 degrees. Turn off overhead lights.
- **Phone or camera at eye level,** horizontal for YouTube, vertical for short form. Clean the lens. Lock focus and exposure.
- **Plain background with some depth.** Move a metre or more away from the wall.
- **Shoot extra:** a wide and a close of each thing, hands doing the work, details, the place, reactions. Hold each shot for 10 seconds with no movement
  at the start and end. Cutaways are what make an edit possible.
- **Film the rehearsal.** A cheap filmed test finds more than a meeting. [practice]
- **Few people in the room,** and coach for comfort. Real people relax when asked real questions, and rarely when handed lines. [practice]
- **One frame rate, one white balance,** set by hand across the whole shoot.

## Common failures

- Shots chosen by subject with no regard for light, so nothing cuts together.
- The same size of shot throughout.
- Slow zooms on everything.
- Heads cut off by a vertical crop or covered by captions.
- Stock footage that looks glossy for an audience that wants real.
- Mixed frame rates and mixed motion styles.
