# Principles for every video

1. **Write down the one sentence the viewer should remember.** Every shot, line and effect either serves it or leaves. Most weak videos
   have no such sentence, so they fill the time with activity.

2. **Give every shot a reason you could say out loud.** "It looks cool" counts only when looking cool is the job, as in a showreel. If
   you cannot say why shot four exists, cut shot four.

3. **When rules conflict, keep them in this order:** emotion, story, rhythm, where the eye is, then screen direction and space. Murch
   weights them 51, 23, 10, 7, 5 and 4 percent, and he says the order matters more than the figures. A cut that feels right and breaks
   continuity is a good cut. A cut that matches perfectly and kills the feeling is a bad one. [practice]

4. **Pace comes from contrast.** A video at one speed feels slow at any speed. Fast passages need slow ones around them, loud needs
   quiet, dense needs empty.

5. **The viewer always knows where to look.** One thing moves or changes at a time. After each cut or move, the eye should land on the
   subject within a moment. The sharpest, brightest, largest or moving thing wins the eye: make that the subject.

6. **Sound quality outranks picture quality.** People forgive soft or plain pictures and leave when speech is hard to hear. Fix the voice
   before the grade. [practice]

7. **Use real things.** Real screens, real numbers, real people, real results. A claim or a statistic comes from the user or a checked
   source. Sample data is labelled "Illustrative data". A missing asset is a question to ask, and a convincing fake is a failure.

8. **The opening keeps the promise.** The title, thumbnail or first line sets an expectation. The first seconds must show the viewer they
   are in the right place. See [structure.md](structure.md).

9. **Respect the viewer's time.** Remove any line or shot that can go without loss. Length is an outcome of the material. A complete
   three-minute video beats a padded twelve-minute one, and a rich forty-minute one can beat both.

10. **Keep one language.** One grade family, one type system, one way of moving, one family of transitions. Mixed languages read as
    amateur even when each piece is good. A change of language is a tool: spend it on a change of meaning.

11. **Judge it where it will be seen.** Check phone videos at phone size, and TV films at full frame. Check the voice on a small speaker.
    Check sound-off platforms with the sound off.

12. **Restraint reads as skill.** Effects, transitions and sound accents earn their place one at a time. When unsure between more and
    less, choose less and look again.

13. **Finished means checked.** A file that rendered proves only that it rendered. See [review.md](review.md).

## Common tells of an amateur edit

- Shots of equal length, one after another.
- Every line illustrated by a clip of the noun just spoken.
- A long logo or a greeting before anything happens.
- Music at one level under the whole film, from first frame to last.
- A different transition on every cut.
- One filter across every shot with no balancing first.
- Text too small for a phone, or gone before it can be read.
- Sound effects on every cut.
- A voice with reverb or heavy compression.
- An ending that winds down and announces itself.

## Common tells of an AI-made video

- Large centred text on a gradient.
- Everything fading in.
- Blue and purple backgrounds with glows.
- Invented product screens and made-up metrics.
- A logo fading out as the only ending.
- Stock clips that never share light or colour.

These appear when decisions were left open, so the model filled them with its safest guess. The brief closes those decisions, and `.claude/skills/unslop/SKILL.md` finds and fixes what remains on a designed frame.
