# Structure: what happens when

## Start from what the viewer already knows

This decides where the video begins. [practice, from Schwartz]

| The viewer | Open with |
|---|---|
| Sees no problem yet | An interesting fact, question or story |
| Feels the problem | The problem, named clearly in their words |
| Knows the options | Why this way is different |
| Knows you and your rivals | Why you |
| Is deciding on price | The offer |

A video must carry people through every stage above where they start. Cold viewers need more steps. Warm viewers leave if you repeat
what they know.

## The opening

- The first seconds confirm the promise of the title, the thumbnail or the feed. If the title asks a question, answer it right away and
  spend the rest of the video on the how. [practice]
- Start on something happening. Cut greetings, logos, "before we start" and any line about the video itself.
- A good opening has three moves: deliver on the click, add one specific reason to stay, then move on with no signpost line. "Let's get
  into it" is a dead seam. [practice]
- Specific beats general. "$5.50 every morning" holds more than "save money on coffee". A curious viewer stays. A confused one leaves.
- Show it where you can. People decide with their eyes first, and many feeds autoplay with no sound.
- Write the first sentence alone, before the rest of the script. Models tend to bury the promise in sentence three.
- Target for long YouTube videos: 60 to 70 percent still watching at 30 seconds. [practice] Compare against the channel's own recent videos.
  The first drop is normal, and a long flat shelf after it is the shape to want.

## The middle

- Keep the viewer knowing what is coming. Raise a problem, resolve it, raise the next. The pace of new problems holds attention far
  more than the pace of cuts. Long, lightly cut videos work when this holds. [practice]
- Test it by stopping at the 1, 2, 3 and 4 minute marks and asking what a viewer expects next. A specific answer passes. "I don't know
  where this is going" fails. [practice]
- Each point in a teaching video has its own small arc: why this matters, the build, the payoff at its end.
- Change something on screen at least every 60 to 90 seconds in a talking video: shot size, a graphic, music stopping, a new place. [practice]
- Put a change of tone where the script changes tone. Picture, music and pace move together.
- Show proof as it comes up, in one line, as part of the teaching. A block of credentials loses people.
- Fix any sag at the script. Effects cannot save a passage with nothing to say.

## The ending

- End when the promise is paid. A wind-down loses viewers. Phrases such as "to recap" and "at the end of the day" cost real audience in
  documented curves. [practice]
- Put the strongest line or image last, and hold it long enough to land.
- One call to action, framed as the natural next step. Name a specific next video when the goal is more viewing.
- A short film can return to its opening image, changed by what happened in between.
- A logo is fine after the point has landed. Keep it short.

## Length

Length is an output of the idea. Estimate speech at 130 to 150 words a minute [default], then read it aloud and time it. Rough ranges for
planning sit in each type file. They are descriptions of common practice and never a quota.

## Writing for the ear

- One idea to a sentence. The ear cannot rewind.
- Read every line aloud. Rewrite what no person would say to another person.
- Delete a sentence and read the passage again. If nothing was lost, it stays deleted.
- Concrete nouns and real numbers. Show the arithmetic for any figure that matters.
- If it feels rushed, cut words before adding time.

## Story shapes to choose from

Pick one and commit.

- **Problem, attempts, solution, result:** teaching and case studies.
- **Before and after:** transformations and products.
- **A real process in its real order:** making, building, how something works. The order is already a plot.
- **Question and answer:** explainers. Answer early at the top level and hold back the depth.
- **Claim, then proof, repeated:** promos and trailers.
- **A character wants something, and something stands in the way:** documentaries, stories and testimonials.
- **Chronology:** histories and journeys. Put a turn in it, or it becomes a list.

Every story needs a turn, a moment where it could go wrong or change direction. Build toward it and let it land.

## How to read persuasion advice

Message design research finds small average effects that often flip between contexts (O'Keefe and Hoeken 2021, 1,149 studies). Treat
sales structures as a list of jobs to do, in a sensible order. No fixed sequence is proven. [measured] Refuting an objection helps. Raising an
objection and leaving it unanswered does harm. [measured]

## After publishing

For a user who publishes on YouTube: an early drop points at the opening; dips point at slow or confusing passages; a big drop at the
end means the video ran past its payoff. The report labels Intros, Top moments, Spikes and Dips. [standard] About 30 percent average view
percentage is realistic for a 6 to 9 minute teaching video. [practice] Shorts loop, so their percentages are a different unit.
